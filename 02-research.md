# 02 — Getting Into Research as an Undergrad

## Why do undergrad research?

- Find out if grad school / a research career is right for you.
- Strong recommendation letters (the #1 factor for PhD/MS admissions).
- Possible publications (workshop paper, poster, or co-authorship).
- Learn to read papers, ask good questions, and work on open-ended problems.

## Path 1: Join a lab at your university

### Step 1 — Find the right people
- Browse your department's faculty pages; list 5–10 professors whose work excites you.
- Read their **recent** papers (last 2–3 years) — not just the abstracts.
- Check if they have PhD students or postdocs; often they'll be your day-to-day mentors.
- Take a course with the professor if you can — the easiest door in.

### Step 2 — Prepare before reaching out
- Have something concrete: relevant coursework, a GitHub repo, a reproduction of a paper's result.
- **Paper reproduction is a superpower:** re-implement a small paper's key experiment and write it up. It proves you can do research-style work.

### Step 3 — The cold email

Keep it short (under 200 words), specific, and easy to say yes to.

```text
Subject: Undergrad interested in [specific topic] research — [Your Name]

Dear Prof. [Name],

I'm a [year] [major] student at [University]. I read your recent paper
"[Title]" and was especially interested in [specific idea/result], because
[one sentence that shows you actually read it].

I have experience with [relevant skills — e.g., PyTorch, data analysis] and
recently [concrete thing: reproduced X / built Y — link]. 

I'd love to contribute to your group's work on [topic], for example by
[small concrete idea or "helping with experiments/data/evaluation"].
I can commit ~[N] hours per week this semester.

Would you be open to a short meeting? My CV and GitHub are attached/linked.

Best regards,
[Name]
[GitHub] · [Website/Blog]
```

**Tips**
- Send to 5–10 people; a low reply rate is normal. Follow up once after ~1–2 weeks.
- Emailing a PhD student in the lab is often more effective than emailing the professor.
- Never send an obviously copy-pasted email to many professors.

### Step 4 — Once you're in
- Show up reliably and communicate progress weekly (even when it's "this didn't work").
- Keep a **research log** (what you tried, results, next steps).
- Ask for a well-scoped first task. Finish it.
- Aim for a concrete output: a workshop paper, a poster at an undergrad symposium, an open-source release of your code.

## Path 2: Structured undergraduate research programs

Check eligibility and deadlines on official sites — many are competitive and some are country-specific:

- **Your university's undergraduate research program** (e.g., summer research internships; at MBZUAI there is the UGRIP undergraduate research internship program)
- **NSF REU** (USA — Research Experiences for Undergraduates; usually US citizens/residents)
- **DAAD RISE** (Germany), **Mitacs Globalink** (Canada), **MITACS / EPFL / ETH summer research programs**, **KAUST VSRP**, **Amgen Scholars**
- Industry research internships (Google, Microsoft Research, Meta, etc. — some have undergrad tracks)

## Path 3: Open research communities (anyone can join)

These communities do real research in the open, often on Discord, and welcome motivated newcomers:

| Community | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| **EleutherAI** | Open LLM research, interpretability, evaluation |
| **Cohere Labs (formerly Cohere For AI)** open science community | Multilingual ML, ML research mentorship |
| **ML Collective** | Open collaboration, mentoring for independent researchers |
| **LAION** | Open datasets and multimodal models |
| **Hugging Face community** | Open-source models, datasets, community sprints |
| **Masakhane** | NLP for African languages |
| **Fast.ai forums** | Practical deep learning, study groups |

How to get involved: lurk, read the project channels, pick a small open task (run an evaluation, clean a dataset, reproduce a result), deliver it, and report back.

## Research skills to build

- **Reading papers:** use the three-pass method (skim → understand figures/method → reproduce details). Keep notes in Zotero/Obsidian.
- **Experiment hygiene:** version control, fixed seeds, config files, experiment tracking (Weights & Biases, MLflow, TensorBoard).
- **Writing:** LaTeX + Overleaf, clear figures, related-work sections.
- **Presenting:** explain your work in 1 minute, 5 minutes, and 20 minutes.

## Research outputs that count

- Workshop papers (top ML conferences have many student-friendly workshops)
- Posters at undergraduate research symposiums
- arXiv preprints (with your advisor's approval)
- Open-source code + model/dataset releases on GitHub / Hugging Face
- Blog posts explaining your research ("paper explainers" get read a lot)
